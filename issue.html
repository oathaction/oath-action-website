Issue brief

Redistricting & Fair Maps

Every ten years, someone redraws the lines that decide which votes count. This brief explains who holds that pen, what reform actually changes, and where the fight stands right now — state by state.

What's at stake
Who decides
In most states, the legislature draws its own district maps. A growing minority hand the job to independent or bipartisan commissions.
Current rule
Maps are redrawn after each decennial census. The party in power often controls the pen — and can lock in an advantage for a decade.
What reform changes
Independent commissions, transparency requirements, and clear map-drawing criteria move the process out of partisan back rooms.
Why it matters
Gerrymandered maps can decide who wins before a single ballot is cast — muting voters and entrenching incumbents.
Timeline
The next nationwide redraw follows the 2030 census, but reform measures and court challenges are live on the ballot in 2026.
The basics

What redistricting actually is

Once a decade, after the census counts everyone, states redraw the boundaries of their legislative and congressional districts. The stated goal is simple: keep each district roughly equal in population so every vote carries similar weight. The reality is anything but simple.

Because most states let elected officials draw their own districts, the people who benefit from the map are usually the ones drawing it. With modern data, mapmakers can predict — almost precinct by precinct — how a district will vote, and shape the lines to "pack" opponents into a few districts or "crack" them across many. The result is a map that decides outcomes before the campaign even begins.

"You don't need to win the most votes to win the most seats. You just need to draw the right lines."
The plain version: Redistricting reform is about who draws the map and by what rules. Take the pen out of the hands of the people whose careers depend on the outcome, and the maps tend to get fairer — and more competitive.
Who holds the levers

Four actors decide your map

Redistricting power isn't held in one place. It passes between the legislature, any commission, the courts, and — through ballot initiatives — the voters themselves. Reform is about shifting weight between these nodes.

How reform happens here

The ballot initiative path

In states where the legislature won't reform itself, voters can. Here's the route a citizen-led map reform travels — from a blank petition to a new commission.

Step 1 · Drafting

File the petition language

Organizers draft the measure and clear legal review, including the single-subject rule that keeps an initiative focused on one issue.

Step 2 · Signatures

Collect signatures statewide

Volunteers and paid circulators gather the required number of valid voter signatures — often hundreds of thousands, with geographic distribution rules.

Step 3 · Qualification

Verify and qualify for the ballot

The secretary of state validates signatures. Survive challenges, and the measure earns a spot on the next general-election ballot.

Step 4 · Campaign

Make the public case

A months-long campaign of canvassing, advertising, and debate — usually against well-funded opposition from the interests the map protects.

Step 5 · Vote

Voters decide

On election day, a simple majority (or supermajority, in some states) enacts the reform directly into law or the state constitution.

Step 6 · Implementation

Stand up the commission

Members are selected, rules are written, and the new body draws its first maps — with watchdogs and courts ready to enforce the standards.
